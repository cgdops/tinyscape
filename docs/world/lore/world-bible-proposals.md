# World Bible Proposals

**Status:** `draft` — three competing directions. Pick one (or a hybrid) before any of it becomes
canon. Nothing here is approved.

Shared constraints all three respect:

- Willowmere is the starting village: timber cottage, market stall, well, pond, footbridge. Small,
  woodland, lived-in.
- The player is an ordinary adventurer with a rucksack, not a chosen one.
- Woodcutting is the first skill going in ([woodcutting-assets.md](../../woodcutting-assets.md)),
  so each direction must make felling a tree feel like it belongs.
- RuneScape Classic / OSRS lineage: a patchwork of small towns, skills as the real content,
  quests as hand-written short stories, humour that is dry rather than zany, and a world you learn
  by walking it rather than by being told about it.

---

## Option A — *The Long Quiet*

**One line:** The apocalypse happened four hundred years ago, everyone survived it, and the world
is now pleasantly overgrown.

**Premise.** The Sundering ended an empire nobody alive remembers fondly. What's left is a
continent of small self-governing villages living comfortably inside the ruins — aqueducts used as
footpaths, a market stall built into a fallen statue's plinth, roads that go somewhere that no
longer exists. Nature took the land back and the survivors made peace with that. Nobody is
rebuilding the empire. Most people would rather it stayed down.

**Tone.** Warm, wry, faintly melancholy. Ruins as furniture, not as horror. The joke is always
that something world-historic is now being used to prop up a fence.

**Magic.** Residual, not practiced. The old empire's works still hum — a well that never runs dry,
a bridge that won't rot, standing stones that hold a charge. Nobody can make new ones. Wizards are
archaeologists with better hats. This keeps magic rare and mysterious without gating early content.

**Factions.** The **Verge Wardens** (rangers who map and contain unstable ruins), the
**Chandlery** (a merchants' league quietly buying up salvage rights), the **Stone-Readers** (a
scholarly order translating the old script, badly and competitively), and dozens of independent
villages who resent all three roughly equally.

**How skills fit.** Every skill is a reclamation skill. Woodcutting clears what the forest took
back. Mining works old imperial seams. Smithing reforges scrap into honest tools. Crafting and
Cooking are the daily business of a village that has to make everything itself. Progression is
"the land becomes usable again," which reads well as a level curve.

**Willowmere's place.** A young village built where an imperial waystation used to be. The well is
one of the old ones — it's why anyone settled here. The footbridge is imperial stonework; the
cottage is a hundred years old at best.

**Quest flavour.** Small, human, with a long shadow behind them. A farmer's plough keeps striking
something. Two villages both claim an aqueduct. A Stone-Reader wants you to fetch a rubbing and is
absolutely lying about why.

**Risk.** "Cosy post-apocalypse" is a crowded shelf. Needs sharp specific detail to avoid feeling
like generic ruins-in-a-forest.

## Option B — *The Shipwright's Coast*

**One line:** A frontier archipelago where every settlement is a going concern, and the real map
is the trade route.

**Premise.** A long ragged coastline and its islands, settled within living memory by people who
came for timber, ore, fish and elbow room. There's no king out here. There are charters, guilds,
harbourmasters and a great deal of arguing about docking fees. The mainland exists, sends
inspectors, and is broadly ignored.

**Tone.** Busy, mercantile, comic. RuneScape Classic's "every town has a shop and an opinion,"
turned up. Stakes are livelihoods, contracts and reputations.

**Magic.** Licensed and mundane-ish. There is a Guild for it, it charges by the hour, and its
practitioners mostly do useful boring work: preserving cargo, charting currents, keeping a lamp lit.
Anything grander is someone else's problem, several islands away.

**Factions.** The **Timber Charter**, the **Deepwater Fishers**, the **Assayers' Guild** (who
certify ore and are relentlessly petty about it), the **Harbour Courts**, and the mainland
**Revenue Office**, universally loathed.

**How skills fit.** The cleanest fit of the three. Skills are *trades*, guilds gate them, and the
economy is the point — you fell timber because a shipwright needs a keel and will pay for it. Level
gates become guild ranks with real social meaning: a rank isn't a number, it's who will now do
business with you. Woodcutting is a founding industry, not a chore.

**Willowmere's place.** An inland timber village a day upriver from the coast. The pond is a
holding pond for floated logs; the footbridge is where the log road crosses. Its whole reason to
exist is sending wood downstream.

**Quest flavour.** Contracts, disputes, sabotage, smuggling, a missing shipment, a guild election.
Adventure is what happens when commerce goes wrong.

**Risk.** Least mysterious. Without a strain of the uncanny somewhere out past the last island,
the world can feel like a spreadsheet with weather.

## Option C — *The Tithe*

**One line:** The land is generous and it keeps accounts.

**Premise.** Everything here grows well, mends easily and yields more than it should — and every
taking is noticed. Fell a tree without leaving something at the stump and the next one comes
harder. This isn't a curse; it's just how the world works, the way rain is. Villages have four
hundred years of etiquette built around it: small offerings, phrases you say, days you don't cut.
Outsiders find it superstitious. Locals find outsiders exhausting.

**Tone.** Warm folk-horror-adjacent, but never cruel. Comfortable, ritual-thick, with something
watching politely from the treeline. Closer to a country parish than a haunted wood.

**Magic.** Ambient and transactional. There are no spellbooks, only the exchange. The "Green
Offices" — hedge-priests, well-keepers, boundary-walkers — are the people who know the correct
forms. Later content is about what happens where the accounts have gone badly wrong.

**Factions.** The **Wardens of the Verge** (keep the boundary hedges), the **Reckoners** (an
order that argues the tithe can be calculated and optimised, which horrifies everyone), the
**Free Cut** (young, pragmatic, cutting without offering, and so far getting away with it), and
village elders who mostly just want everyone to stop.

**How skills fit.** Distinctive and mechanically interesting: every gathering skill has a give as
well as a take, and skill mastery is partly about *knowing the right form* — cheaper offerings,
better yields, access to groves that won't tolerate the careless. Woodcutting becomes a small
ceremony, which is a strong first impression. Also the highest design risk: an offering cost
attached to gathering can read as a tax if tuned badly.

**Willowmere's place.** An old, correct, slightly smug village that has kept the forms for
generations and has the healthy woodland to show for it. The well is a shrine as much as a well.
The market stall sells offering goods. Someone in town is quietly with the Free Cut.

**Quest flavour.** A debt coming due. A neighbour cutting corners. A grove that has stopped
accepting. Deciding whether the old forms are wisdom or habit — and the game not telling you.

**Risk.** Needs careful tuning so ritual feels like flavour and mastery, not friction. Also the
hardest to keep light-hearted.

---

## Recommendation

**Option B as the world's skeleton, Option C as its weather.** The guild-and-trade frame gives
skills a reason to exist, gates that mean something socially, and endless small quests — the
Classic feel, and the safest structure to build systems on. Option C supplies what B lacks: a
reason the deep woods and the far islands feel *older* than the people working them, without
committing every gathering action to a ritual cost. Option A's ruins can then sit in the
background as a third layer if the world ever needs history.

If a single pure direction is preferred, **B** is the safest and **C** the most distinctive.

3. Is Willowmere inland-timber (B), imperial waystation (A), or old-and-correct (C)?
4. How much does the woodcutting first-skill experience carry the world's tone?
